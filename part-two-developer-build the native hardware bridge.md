RSIS 4.1 — Bluetooth Safety Presence Beacon (SPB) & Voluntary Enrollment ProtocolIntroduction: 
Explaining the Idea in Plain TermsRSIS 4.1 is an upgrade designed to extend road safety beyond existing users and 
quietly protect people who have never installed RSIS at all. In everyday terms, this means that a driver using RSIS 
can help warn a nearby pedestrian—even one who has never heard of RSIS—without knowing who that person is, without 
sending them a message, and without collecting any personal data. The system works more like a digital safety signal 
than a communication tool. Just as a flashing crosswalk sign warns everyone nearby without asking who they are,
RSIS 4.1 broadcasts a short-range Bluetooth safety presence that nearby phones can detect if—and only if—the phone’s
owner chooses to engage.At its simplest level, RSIS 4.1 allows safety information to exist in the environment itself.
Instead of requiring every person on the road to already be a registered user, RSIS devices act as safety nodes that 
announce, “a potentially risky movement is nearby.” This announcement does not target anyone, does not identify anyone, 
and does not demand action. It merely creates an opportunity for nearby devices to opt into temporary protection. 
This approach is what makes the upgrade both effective and legal.From Users to the Environment: 
The Safety Presence ConceptTraditional apps communicate directly with users: notifications, 
messages, invites, and alerts. RSIS 4.1 deliberately avoids this model when dealing with non-users. 
Instead, every active RSIS device periodically emits a Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) advertisement
known as a Safety Presence Beacon (SPB). This beacon is not a message and cannot carry text, links, 
or instructions. It is simply a structured signal that indicates the presence of a moving participant, 
their general role (such as driver or cyclist), and whether their current motion suggests elevated risk.
In layman’s terms, the beacon acts like a digital flare. It does not say who is holding it, where they are going, 
or where they came from. It only says that “something nearby may matter for safety right now.” Because Bluetooth 
is short-range, this signal only exists within the physical space where risk is relevant—crosswalks, parking lots, 
intersections, and shared streets. Once devices move apart, the signal disappears completely.How a Non-User Experiences 
RSIS 4.1For a pedestrian who does not have RSIS installed, nothing happens by default. Their phone continues operating normally.
If their operating system passively detects an RSIS Safety Presence Beacon and determines it qualifies as a safety-related broadcast, 
the phone may display a system-level notice such as “Nearby road safety signal detected.” This notice is generated by the operating 
system, not by RSIS, and only appears if the user taps to learn more.If the pedestrian chooses to engage, they are shown a simple 
explanation: a nearby vehicle or cyclist is broadcasting an anonymous safety signal, and the pedestrian may temporarily enable 
Bluetooth-based hazard alerts. At this point, consent is explicit and voluntary. No account is created, no app is installed,
and no personal data is exchanged. The pedestrian’s phone simply agrees to listen for nearby safety beacons for a limited time
and generate local alerts if risk increases.Temporary Pedestrian Safety ModeOnce enabled, the pedestrian’s device enters a 
temporary safety mode. In this mode, the phone performs local calculations based on Bluetooth signal strength changes, timing, 
and motion correlation. If a nearby RSIS user is approaching at a speed or trajectory that statistically increases collision risk,
the pedestrian receives a subtle alert—such as a vibration or short audio tone—indicating heightened danger. Importantly, this alert 
does not reveal who the other participant is, where they are exactly, or what they are doing beyond the risk itself.This safety mode 
is intentionally limited. It automatically expires after a short period, stores no history, and can be turned off instantly.
The pedestrian’s device does not transmit anything back to RSIS during this phase. Safety is delivered first, silently and 
respectfully, without turning the pedestrian into a tracked participant.Ethical and Legal Enrollment PathOnly after safety 
value has been demonstrated does RSIS 4.1 present an optional invitation to install the full RSIS application. 
This invitation appears once and only once, typically after a safety event or when the temporary mode expires. 
The message is informational rather than promotional, explaining that installing RSIS allows the same protection
to be active anytime, even without nearby users.This approach is fundamentally different from viral marketing or 
spam-based growth loops. The system does not contact the pedestrian unless they initiate interaction. It does not
use their contacts, identifiers, or location history. From a legal standpoint, RSIS is broadcasting a public 
safety signal, not soliciting users. From an ethical standpoint, trust is earned through demonstrated protection 
rather than persuasion.Technical Structure of the Safety Presence BeaconAt a deeper technical level, the Safety
Presence Beacon is a compact Bluetooth Low Energy advertisement designed to minimize data while maximizing safety relevance.
A typical packet structure is shown below:

[ RSIS-SPB HEADER ]     2 bytes
[ PROTOCOL VERSION ]   1 byte
[ ROLE FLAG ]          1 byte
[ RISK STATE ]         1 byte
[ MOTION CLASS ]       1 byte
[ EPHEMERAL TOKEN ]    8 bytes
[ CHECKSUM ]           1 byte

Each field is carefully constrained. The role flag indicates whether the broadcaster is a driver, cyclist, walker, or passenger. The risk state communicates urgency without explanation. The motion class provides a coarse indication of movement intensity. The ephemeral token rotates frequently and is never linked to the user’s Pseudo Safety Token (PST), ensuring the broadcast cannot be used for tracking. No GPS coordinates, identifiers, or persistent values are included.Integration with the RSIS Hybrid ArchitectureWithin the RSIS 4.1 hybrid system, the mobile JavaScript layer manages Bluetooth broadcasting, consent presentation, and alert delivery, while the Crow C++ engine performs rapid proximity and collision-risk calculations. When GPS or internet connectivity is unavailable, the system relies entirely on Bluetooth-based inference, allowing safety alerts to function in tunnels, dense urban areas, or emergency conditions. When connectivity is restored, only high-level, anonymized learning signals may be shared—never raw encounter data.The result is a system that treats connectivity and participation as enhancements rather than requirements. RSIS 4.1 preserves the original privacy-first philosophy while extending protection outward into the surrounding environment.Summary: From App to Safety InfrastructureRSIS 4.1 transforms RSIS from a tool that protects only its users into a distributed safety presence that benefits everyone nearby. By using Bluetooth as a passive, anonymous hazard signal and placing consent firmly in the hands of non-users, the system achieves broader protection without surveillance, spam, or coercion. Each paragraph of the system—from layman explanation to packet-level design—reinforces the same principle: safety information should be available when it matters, identity should remain private, and participation should always be voluntary. One-Time Invitation and Spam Prevention Mechanism (Anonymous User Status)The system prevents repeated notices and spam to non-users while maintaining complete anonymity through strict local-only logic and one-time triggers.Core PrinciplesNo persistent identifiers are ever created or stored for non-users.
All state is kept locally on the device using simple boolean flags.
The invitation is shown once and only once per device.
No data is sent to any server until (and unless) the user voluntarily installs the full app.

How One-Time Consent Invitation WorksWhen a non-user first engages with Temporary Safety Mode:The device checks a local flag (hasSeenRSISInvitation).
If the flag is false, the system allows normal operation of temporary mode.
After temporary mode expires or after a safety alert is delivered, the system shows the one-time informational invitation.
Immediately after showing (or dismissing) the invitation, the device sets hasSeenRSISInvitation = true.
On all future encounters, the system checks the flag first. If true, the invitation is skipped entirely.

This logic runs entirely inside the temporary safety mode session and requires no account, no user ID, and no server communication.Anonymous Code Logic (Pseudocode)pseudocode

// Runs locally on non-user device during Temporary Safety Mode

function onTemporaryModeStarted() {
    // Check local anonymous flag only (no user ID involved)
    if (localStorage.get("hasSeenRSISInvitation") == true) {
        // Already shown once — do nothing
        return;
    }
    
    // Normal temporary safety mode operation continues...
    startLocalBLEScanning();
    monitorRiskAndAlert();
}

function onTemporaryModeEnded() {
    if (localStorage.get("hasSeenRSISInvitation") == false) {
        // Show invitation exactly once
        showOneTimeInformationalMessage(
            "You received protection from a nearby safety signal. " +
            "Install RSIS for always-on protection."
        );
        
        // Set flag immediately — prevents any future invitations
        localStorage.set("hasSeenRSISInvitation", true);
    }
    
    stopLocalBLEScanning();
    // All temporary data is discarded
}

Key Anonymity and Anti-Spam ProtectionsZero identifiers: Only a simple boolean is stored locally. No device ID, no token, no profile.
Session-based: The flag only exists while the user has engaged temporary mode.
One-time enforcement: Once set to true, the invitation branch is permanently skipped on that device.
No server involvement: The flag and invitation logic never touch the Base44 backend.
Automatic expiration: Temporary mode itself has a hard time limit, further limiting exposure.
OS-level protection: The initial system notice is controlled by iOS/Android, which already rate-limits similar notifications.
